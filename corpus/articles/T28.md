Merck, Moderna say personalized mRNA vaccine met goals in late-stage melanoma trial

RAHWAY, N.J. (Reuters) — Moderna and Merck said on Wednesday that a personalized mRNA cancer vaccine reduced the risk of melanoma returning or spreading in a late-stage trial, a milestone in a new field of cancer treatment that sent Moderna's shares surging as much as 160%.

The companies announced positive topline results from the Phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial evaluating intismeran autogene, an mRNA-based individualized neoantigen therapy, in combination with Merck's immunotherapy Keytruda (pembrolizumab), compared with Keytruda alone.

At a pre-specified interim analysis, the combination demonstrated statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in the primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival (RFS) and the key secondary endpoint of distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) in patients with completely resected stage IIB-IV cutaneous melanoma who had not received prior systemic therapy.

The trial enrolled 1,137 patients, randomized 2:1 to receive intismeran, dosed every three weeks for up to nine doses, with Keytruda every six weeks for up to nine cycles, versus Keytruda alone for approximately one year. The study will continue to evaluate other key endpoints, including overall survival.

This is the first positive Phase 3 readout for an individualized neoantigen therapy and for an mRNA-based cancer therapy, the companies said. The data will be presented at an upcoming international medical meeting, and the partners plan to engage with regulators on filing submissions.

Moderna President Stephen Hoge said thousands of patients who have undergone surgery to remove high-risk melanoma tumors could benefit as soon as next year if regulators approve the vaccine.

The Phase 3 results build on five-year follow-up data from the Phase 2b KEYNOTE-942 trial, presented at the 2026 ASCO Annual Meeting, in which the combination reduced the risk of recurrence or death by 49% and the risk of distant metastasis or death by 59% compared with Keytruda alone.

Merck and Moderna have been research partners since 2016. Merck paid $200 million upfront to begin the alliance and $250 million in 2022 to exercise its option for intismeran.
