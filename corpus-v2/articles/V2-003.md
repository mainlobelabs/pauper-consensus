Annular solar eclipse visible from Antarctica for first time since 2014
Wikipedia Current events portal, February 17, 2026 (via EclipseWise, AP)

An annular solar eclipse lasting up to 2 minutes and 20 seconds was visible from Antarctica on Tuesday, February 17, 2026, the first annular eclipse seen from the continent since 2014, with additional visibility from the Southern and South Indian Oceans and partial views in parts of Africa, Argentina, and Chile.

The eclipse reached a magnitude of 0.963, meaning the moon covered nearly all of the sun's disk at maximum, leaving a bright ring of daylight around its silhouette. Maximum annularity occurred at 12:11 UT and lasted 2 minutes and 20 seconds for observers beneath the path.

The annular path crossed Antarctica only, making the event a rare sighting for the southern high latitudes. Elsewhere, the eclipse was partial, with the best views from southern Africa and the southern tips of Argentina and Chile, where a substantial portion of the sun was covered at the moment of maximum eclipse.

The ring-of-fire effect occurs when the moon is near the far end of its orbit and appears too small in the sky to cover the sun completely. Astronomers describe such events as among the most photogenic in the solar calendar.

The eclipse follows the annular eclipse of October 14, 2023, which was visible from the southern Pacific and parts of Australia. Observers at the continent's research stations and in coastal Southern Hemisphere locations were among those positioned to watch the event, which eclipse-tracking services had flagged in advance as a notable viewing opportunity for the region.
